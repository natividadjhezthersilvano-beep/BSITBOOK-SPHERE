Overview

Cybersecurity, privacy, and computer ethics are essential areas of information technology that focus on protecting digital systems, safeguarding personal information, and promoting responsible behavior when using computers and the internet. As technology becomes part of everyday life, individuals and organizations increasingly depend on digital systems to store, process, and exchange information.

Computers and networks are used for online banking, electronic payments, education, communication, healthcare, business operations, entertainment, and government services. Although these technologies provide convenience and efficiency, they also create risks such as hacking, identity theft, malware infections, privacy violations, online scams, and unauthorized access to confidential information.

Cybersecurity focuses on defending digital systems against attacks. Data privacy focuses on protecting an individual's personal information and controlling how it is processed. Computer ethics establishes moral principles that guide people in using technology properly and respecting the rights of others.

Understanding these concepts helps users become more responsible digital citizens and prepares IT professionals to develop and maintain secure, trustworthy, and ethical information systems.

Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, servers, mobile devices, networks, applications, and electronic information from cyberattacks, unauthorized access, damage, disruption, and theft. It combines technologies, security policies, procedures, and user awareness to reduce digital risks.

Cybersecurity is not limited to installing antivirus software. It also includes protecting network connections, securing applications, managing user permissions, monitoring suspicious activities, backing up information, and responding to security incidents.

Importance of Cybersecurity

  • Protection of Information: Prevents unauthorized individuals from accessing confidential documents, personal records, and business information.
  • Prevention of Financial Loss: Helps protect online banking accounts, payment information, and financial transactions from fraud and theft.
  • System Reliability: Reduces the possibility of attacks that interrupt computer systems, websites, applications, and network services.
  • Privacy Protection: Helps prevent the unauthorized collection, exposure, and misuse of personal data.
  • Business Continuity: Helps organizations continue operating even when security incidents occur.
  • Customer Trust: Demonstrates that an organization takes the protection of customer information seriously.
  • Legal Compliance: Helps organizations meet applicable laws, regulations, and contractual security requirements.

Major Areas of Cybersecurity

  • Network Security: Protects computer networks from unauthorized access, malicious traffic, and network-based attacks.
  • Application Security: Protects software and applications against vulnerabilities that attackers may exploit.
  • Information Security: Protects information from unauthorized access, modification, disclosure, and loss.
  • Endpoint Security: Protects devices such as desktop computers, laptops, smartphones, and servers.
  • Cloud Security: Protects information, applications, and services stored or operated through cloud computing platforms.
  • Operational Security: Focuses on policies and procedures for handling and protecting important information.

Example of Cybersecurity in Practice

When a student logs in to a school learning management system, the system checks the student's credentials, verifies access permissions, and protects the connection. Security measures help prevent other people from viewing the student's private records or changing submitted academic information.

Goals of Information Security

Information security aims to protect information throughout its lifecycle, including collection, storage, transmission, use, and disposal. One of its most widely used models is the CIA Triad, which consists of Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality ensures that information is available only to individuals, applications, or systems that have been authorized to access it. It prevents sensitive information from being disclosed to unauthorized users.

Confidentiality can be maintained through passwords, encryption, access permissions, authentication, and proper information handling procedures.

Examples:

  • A student can only access their own private student account.
  • An employee cannot open confidential files without permission.
  • A hospital limits access to patient records to authorized healthcare personnel.

Integrity

Integrity ensures that information remains accurate, complete, consistent, and trustworthy. It prevents unauthorized changes, deletion, or alteration of data.

Organizations use permissions, audit logs, checksums, digital signatures, and validation procedures to help maintain data integrity.

Examples:

  • A banking system prevents unauthorized modification of account balances.
  • A school database records changes made to student grades.
  • A downloaded file can be checked against a published hash value to detect unexpected changes.

Availability

Availability ensures that information, applications, and systems are accessible to authorized users when needed. It involves maintaining system performance and preventing unnecessary interruptions.

Availability can be improved through regular maintenance, backups, redundant systems, monitoring, disaster recovery plans, and protection against denial-of-service attacks.

Examples:

  • An online enrollment system remains accessible during enrollment periods.
  • A business maintains backup internet connections to reduce service interruptions.
  • A hospital maintains access to essential digital systems during emergencies.

Additional Information Security Principles

  • Authentication: Confirms the identity of users or systems.
  • Authorization: Determines what an authenticated user is permitted to access or perform.
  • Accountability: Ensures that activities can be traced to the responsible user or system.
  • Non-repudiation: Provides evidence that a particular action or transaction occurred and helps prevent someone from falsely denying their participation.

Cybersecurity Threats

A cybersecurity threat is a potential danger that can exploit weaknesses in computer systems, applications, networks, or human behavior. A threat may result in unauthorized access, information theft, data destruction, financial loss, or interruption of services.

Common Cybersecurity Threats

  • Hacking: Attempting to gain unauthorized access to computers, networks, accounts, or information systems. Some security professionals also perform authorized testing to identify weaknesses.
  • Malware: Malicious software created to damage devices, steal information, monitor users, or gain unauthorized access.
  • Phishing: Using fraudulent emails, messages, or websites to trick people into revealing sensitive information.
  • Ransomware: Malicious software that encrypts or blocks access to information and demands payment.
  • Denial-of-Service Attack: Attempts to make a website, network, or online service unavailable by overwhelming it with traffic or requests.
  • Distributed Denial-of-Service Attack: A denial-of-service attack carried out using multiple compromised devices or systems.
  • Password Attacks: Attempts to steal, guess, or discover passwords to access protected accounts.
  • Identity Theft: The unauthorized use of another person's personal information, often to commit fraud.
  • Insider Threat: A security risk caused by someone with authorized access, such as an employee or contractor, who intentionally or accidentally exposes information.
  • Man-in-the-Middle Attack: An attacker secretly intercepts or potentially alters communication between two parties.

Vulnerabilities and Exploits

A vulnerability is a weakness in software, hardware, network configuration, or security procedures. An exploit is a method or piece of code that takes advantage of a vulnerability. Attackers may use exploits to gain access, execute unauthorized actions, or disrupt systems.

Regular security updates, system testing, vulnerability assessments, and secure configuration practices help reduce these risks.

Possible Effects of Cyberattacks

  • Loss or theft of confidential information.
  • Financial damage to individuals and organizations.
  • Interruption of business and school operations.
  • Damage to an organization's reputation.
  • Unauthorized use of personal accounts.
  • Loss of customer and public trust.

Malware and Malicious Software

Malware is short for malicious software. It refers to programs or code intentionally designed to damage devices, disrupt operations, collect information, or gain unauthorized access. Malware may infect computers through unsafe downloads, malicious attachments, compromised websites, removable devices, and vulnerable applications.

Types of Malware

  • Computer Virus: A program that attaches itself to files or applications and spreads when the infected content is executed.
  • Computer Worm: Malware that can reproduce and spread across computers or networks without requiring the user to manually run an infected file.
  • Trojan Horse: Malicious software disguised as a legitimate or useful application.
  • Ransomware: Malware that locks a device or encrypts files and demands payment to restore access.
  • Spyware: Software that secretly monitors user activities and collects information.
  • Adware: Software that displays unwanted advertisements and may track browsing behavior.
  • Rootkit: Malware designed to hide malicious activities and maintain unauthorized access to a system.
  • Keylogger: Software or hardware that records keyboard input and may capture passwords or other sensitive information.
  • Botnet Malware: Malware that compromises devices and allows attackers to control them as part of a larger network of infected devices.

How Malware Spreads

  • Opening infected email attachments.
  • Downloading applications from untrusted websites.
  • Installing pirated or modified software.
  • Visiting compromised websites.
  • Connecting infected USB drives.
  • Exploiting outdated operating systems and applications.

Signs of a Possible Malware Infection

  • Unexpected pop-up advertisements.
  • Unusual computer slowdown or crashes.
  • Unknown applications appearing on a device.
  • Unexpected changes to browser settings.
  • Unusual network or account activity.
  • Files becoming inaccessible or unexpectedly renamed.

Ways to Prevent Malware

  • Install reputable antivirus or endpoint security software.
  • Keep operating systems and applications updated.
  • Avoid downloading files from unknown sources.
  • Do not open suspicious email attachments.
  • Scan removable storage devices before opening files.
  • Use standard accounts for everyday activities when possible.
  • Maintain regular backups of important information.

Phishing and Social Engineering

Phishing is a cyberattack in which attackers pretend to represent trusted organizations, businesses, schools, or individuals. They use fraudulent messages, websites, or phone calls to convince people to disclose passwords, bank information, verification codes, or other confidential data.

Social engineering is a broader form of manipulation that takes advantage of human emotions and behavior. Attackers may create a sense of urgency, fear, authority, curiosity, or trust to influence their targets.

Types of Phishing

  • Email Phishing: Fraudulent emails that imitate banks, online services, schools, or companies.
  • Spear Phishing: Personalized attacks directed toward a particular individual or organization.
  • Whaling: Targeted phishing that focuses on senior executives or other high-profile individuals.
  • Smishing: Phishing performed through SMS or text messages.
  • Vishing: Fraudulent phone calls used to obtain confidential information.
  • Clone Phishing: A fraudulent copy of a legitimate message containing a malicious link or attachment.
  • Website Spoofing: Fake websites designed to look like legitimate websites to steal login credentials or financial information.

Common Social Engineering Techniques

  • Pretexting: Creating a false story or identity to convince someone to reveal information.
  • Baiting: Offering something attractive, such as free software or a prize, to trick a user into taking an unsafe action.
  • Quid Pro Quo: Promising a service or benefit in exchange for information or access.
  • Impersonation: Pretending to be a trusted person, company representative, or technical support worker.

Warning Signs of Phishing

  • Messages that demand immediate action.
  • Unexpected requests for passwords or verification codes.
  • Suspicious links and unfamiliar website addresses.
  • Unexpected attachments from unknown senders.
  • Spelling errors or unusual sender addresses.
  • Unexpected prizes, rewards, or financial offers.

How to Avoid Phishing

  • Verify the sender through an official communication channel.
  • Check website addresses carefully before logging in.
  • Never share passwords or one-time verification codes.
  • Do not open unexpected attachments.
  • Report suspicious messages to the appropriate organization.
  • Use multi-factor authentication where available.

Security Measures

Security measures are methods, tools, and procedures used to protect information systems from threats. They help prevent unauthorized access, identify suspicious activities, limit damage, and restore normal operations after an incident.

Technical Security Measures

  • Antivirus Software: Detects, blocks, and removes many types of malicious software.
  • Firewall: Monitors and controls incoming and outgoing network traffic based on security rules.
  • Encryption: Protects information by converting readable data into an encoded form.
  • Intrusion Detection System: Monitors network or system activities for signs of suspicious behavior.
  • Intrusion Prevention System: Detects and attempts to block malicious network activities.
  • Multi-Factor Authentication: Requires multiple verification factors to access an account.
  • Backup Systems: Preserve additional copies of information for recovery.

Administrative Security Measures

  • Establishing clear information security policies.
  • Providing cybersecurity training for employees.
  • Limiting access according to job responsibilities.
  • Performing regular security assessments.
  • Creating incident response and recovery procedures.

Physical Security Measures

  • Using locks and access cards in server rooms.
  • Installing security cameras in restricted areas.
  • Preventing unauthorized physical access to computers.
  • Protecting equipment against theft and environmental damage.
  • Properly disposing of storage devices containing sensitive data.

Security Updates and Patch Management

Software updates and security patches correct errors, improve performance, and fix known vulnerabilities. Organizations should regularly identify outdated software and apply appropriate updates after considering compatibility and operational needs.

Authentication and Access Control

Authentication is the process of verifying the identity of a person, device, or system before granting access. Access control determines which resources an authenticated user may view, modify, or use.

Authentication Factors

  • Something You Know: A password, PIN, or security answer.
  • Something You Have: A security token, registered mobile device, or authentication key.
  • Something You Are: A physical characteristic such as a fingerprint or facial feature.

Authentication Methods

  • Password: A secret combination of characters.
  • PIN: A personal identification number.
  • Biometrics: Fingerprint or facial recognition.
  • Security Token: A physical or digital authentication item.
  • Multi-Factor Authentication: Uses two or more different authentication factors.
  • Passkeys: Passwordless credentials that use cryptographic keys and can be protected by a device's biometric or PIN verification.

Access Control Models

  • Discretionary Access Control: The owner of a resource decides which users can access it.
  • Mandatory Access Control: Access is determined by centrally defined security classifications and policies.
  • Role-Based Access Control: Permissions are assigned according to a user's organizational role.
  • Attribute-Based Access Control: Access decisions depend on attributes such as user identity, resource type, location, and conditions.

Principle of Least Privilege

The principle of least privilege means that users should receive only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their tasks. This reduces the risk of accidental changes, unauthorized access, and misuse of important information.

For example, a student may view their grades, while only authorized teachers and administrators can enter or modify official academic records.

Encryption and Data Protection

Encryption is the process of converting readable information, known as plaintext, into an unreadable format called ciphertext. The process uses an algorithm and a key to protect the information. Decryption converts ciphertext back into readable information for an authorized recipient.

Types of Encryption

  • Symmetric Encryption: Uses the same secret key to encrypt and decrypt information. It is generally efficient for protecting large amounts of data.
  • Asymmetric Encryption: Uses a public key and a private key. One key is used for operations such as encryption or verification, while the corresponding key performs the matching decryption or signing operation.

Encryption in Everyday Technology

  • HTTPS: Uses TLS to protect communication between a browser and a website.
  • Secure Messaging: Some messaging services use end-to-end encryption to protect message contents.
  • File Encryption: Protects documents stored on computers and external storage devices.
  • Online Banking: Uses encrypted connections to help protect information exchanged during transactions.
  • Device Encryption: Helps prevent unauthorized people from reading data stored on a lost or stolen device.

Importance of Encryption

  • Protects confidential information.
  • Reduces the risk of unauthorized reading of data.
  • Helps secure communication over networks.
  • Supports privacy in digital transactions.
  • Can help verify the authenticity and integrity of information when used with appropriate cryptographic methods.

Encryption and Hashing

Encryption and hashing are different security techniques. Encryption is designed to be reversible when the correct key is available. Hashing transforms data into a fixed-length output and is generally designed to be one-way.

Secure password storage normally uses dedicated password-hashing algorithms with unique salts and appropriate computational settings rather than storing passwords as readable text.

Data Privacy

Data privacy refers to the proper collection, processing, use, storage, sharing, retention, and disposal of personal information. It focuses on protecting an individual's privacy and ensuring that information is handled in a lawful, transparent, and responsible manner.

Data privacy is important because personal information can be misused for identity theft, fraud, unauthorized tracking, discrimination, unwanted marketing, and other harmful activities.

Important Principles of Data Privacy

  • Transparency: Individuals should receive clear information about how their personal data is collected and processed.
  • Legitimate Purpose: Personal information should be processed for a specified and lawful purpose.
  • Proportionality: The information collected should be adequate, relevant, and not excessive for the intended purpose.
  • Accuracy: Personal information should be kept accurate and updated when necessary.
  • Retention Limitation: Personal information should not be kept longer than necessary, subject to legal requirements.
  • Security: Appropriate safeguards should protect information against unauthorized access, loss, destruction, or disclosure.

Importance of Data Privacy

  • Protects individuals from misuse of personal information.
  • Helps prevent identity theft and online fraud.
  • Maintains confidentiality in personal and professional activities.
  • Promotes trust between users and organizations.
  • Encourages responsible collection and processing of information.
  • Supports compliance with data protection laws.

Data Privacy and Data Security

Data privacy and data security are related but different concepts. Data privacy concerns whether personal information is collected and used appropriately. Data security concerns the safeguards used to protect information from unauthorized access, alteration, loss, or destruction.

For example, a school may use encryption and access controls to secure student records. Its privacy practices determine why the records are collected, who may use them, and when they should be deleted or retained.

Personal Information

Personal information refers to information that identifies an individual or can reasonably be linked to that individual. It may be collected through forms, online accounts, mobile applications, school systems, business transactions, and government services.

Examples of Personal Information

  • Full name
  • Home or mailing address
  • Email address
  • Telephone or mobile number
  • Date of birth
  • Student or employee identification details
  • Account identifiers and usernames
  • Photographs that identify an individual

Sensitive Personal Information

Sensitive personal information is a legally defined category that receives additional protection under applicable privacy laws. Under the Philippine Data Privacy Act, it includes information about an individual's race or ethnic origin, marital status, age, color, religious or philosophical affiliation, health, education, genetic or sexual life, certain legal proceedings, and government-issued information specified by the law.

Personal Information in Digital Systems

Digital systems may collect personal information when users register accounts, complete online forms, purchase products, access educational platforms, or use mobile applications. Organizations should explain the purpose of collection and apply appropriate safeguards.

Examples in Different Organizations

  • Schools: Student names, enrollment records, grades, and contact details.
  • Banks: Account information, transaction records, and identity verification details.
  • Hospitals: Patient identification and medical records.
  • Online Stores: Customer names, delivery addresses, and order information.
  • Government Agencies: Identification details and records used to provide public services.

Online Privacy Risks

Online privacy risks occur when personal information is exposed, collected without proper authority, or used in ways that individuals do not expect or approve. These risks may come from cybercriminals, careless users, insecure systems, or improper organizational practices.

Common Online Privacy Risks

  • Identity Theft: Personal information is used by another person to impersonate an individual.
  • Data Breach: Personal or confidential information is accessed, disclosed, altered, or stolen without authorization.
  • Online Tracking: Websites and services may collect information about browsing activities and user behavior.
  • Oversharing: Posting too much personal information online can expose individuals to fraud, harassment, and unwanted contact.
  • Unsecured Public Wi-Fi: Poorly protected networks may expose users to interception and other security threats.
  • Unauthorized Data Sharing: Personal information may be disclosed without an appropriate legal basis or proper transparency.
  • Location Tracking: Applications and services may collect location information through device permissions and other technologies.
  • Account Takeover: Attackers gain control of another person's online account and may access private messages, files, and personal information.

Consequences of Privacy Violations

  • Financial loss and fraudulent transactions.
  • Exposure of private photographs and documents.
  • Damage to personal or professional reputation.
  • Unwanted messages, harassment, or impersonation.
  • Loss of trust in digital platforms and organizations.

Data Protection Practices

Data protection practices help individuals and organizations keep information safe throughout its lifecycle. They include technical controls, responsible data handling, secure storage, access management, and appropriate disposal.

Ways to Protect Personal Information

  • Use strong and unique passwords for different accounts.
  • Enable multi-factor authentication whenever available.
  • Never share passwords or verification codes.
  • Review privacy settings on social media accounts.
  • Avoid posting confidential personal information publicly.
  • Use secure websites when submitting sensitive information.
  • Keep devices and applications updated.
  • Back up important files regularly.
  • Be careful when connecting to public Wi-Fi networks.
  • Review application permissions before granting access.
  • Delete information securely when it is no longer needed.

Responsible Data Handling in Organizations

  • Collect only information necessary for a specified purpose.
  • Explain why personal information is being collected.
  • Limit access to authorized personnel.
  • Use appropriate technical and organizational safeguards.
  • Keep personal information only as long as necessary or required by applicable law.
  • Establish procedures for handling privacy complaints and security incidents.
  • Provide appropriate training to employees who handle personal information.

Data Backup and Recovery

Data backup involves creating additional copies of important information so that it can be restored after accidental deletion, hardware failure, ransomware attacks, or other incidents.

  • Full Backup: Copies all selected information.
  • Incremental Backup: Copies information changed since the most recent backup.
  • Differential Backup: Copies information changed since the last full backup.

Backups should be protected against unauthorized access and periodically tested to confirm that information can actually be restored.

Data Privacy Act of 2012

Republic Act No. 10173, also known as the Data Privacy Act of 2012, is a Philippine law that protects the fundamental human right to privacy while ensuring the free flow of information for innovation, growth, and development.

The law establishes rules and responsibilities for the processing of personal information. It applies to personal information controllers and personal information processors, subject to the law's scope and applicable exceptions.

National Privacy Commission

The National Privacy Commission (NPC) is an independent body that administers and implements the Data Privacy Act of 2012. It promotes the protection of personal information and helps ensure that organizations follow applicable data privacy requirements.

Objectives of the Data Privacy Act

  • Protect the privacy rights of individuals.
  • Protect personal information in information systems.
  • Promote responsible processing of personal data.
  • Prevent unauthorized disclosure and misuse of information.
  • Establish responsibilities for organizations handling data.
  • Provide mechanisms for addressing privacy complaints.

Important Terms Under the Law

  • Data Subject: An individual whose personal information is processed.
  • Personal Information Controller: A person or organization that controls the collection, holding, processing, or use of personal information, or instructs another party to process it on its behalf.
  • Personal Information Processor: A person or organization that processes personal information on behalf of a personal information controller.
  • Processing: Operations performed on personal information, such as collection, recording, organization, storage, use, modification, retrieval, disclosure, blocking, erasure, or destruction.

Rights of Data Subjects

  • Right to Be Informed: The individual has the right to know whether personal information will be collected or processed and to receive relevant information about that processing.
  • Right to Access: The individual may request reasonable access to their personal information.
  • Right to Object: The individual may object to certain types of processing under conditions provided by law.
  • Right to Erasure or Blocking: The individual may request the blocking, removal, or destruction of personal information in circumstances provided by law.
  • Right to Rectification: The individual may request correction of inaccurate or incomplete personal data.
  • Right to File a Complaint: The individual may raise a complaint with the National Privacy Commission when privacy rights are violated.
  • Right to Damages: The individual may seek compensation when legally entitled.
  • Right to Data Portability: The individual may obtain a copy of personal data in an appropriate electronic or structured format when the legal conditions are met.

Examples of Data Privacy in Schools

  • Keeping student records accessible only to authorized staff.
  • Protecting student grades and examination results.
  • Securing online enrollment and student information systems.
  • Preventing unauthorized disclosure of student contact details.
  • Using appropriate permissions before publishing student photos.
  • Following retention and disposal procedures for student records.

Personal Data Breaches

A personal data breach is a security incident that may result in the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data.

Organizations should have procedures to detect, investigate, contain, and document personal data breaches. The Data Privacy Act's implementing rules and applicable NPC regulations establish notification requirements for qualifying incidents. Certain reportable breaches must be notified to the NPC and affected data subjects within 72 hours from knowledge or reasonable belief of the breach, subject to applicable rules and exceptions.

Computer Ethics

Computer ethics refers to the moral principles and standards that guide people's behavior when using computers, the internet, software, and information technology. It helps users understand their responsibilities when accessing, creating, sharing, and managing digital information.

Computer ethics applies to students, teachers, programmers, IT professionals, business owners, government employees, and ordinary technology users. It encourages honesty, respect, fairness, responsibility, and proper use of digital resources.

Fundamental Principles of Computer Ethics

  • Respect for Privacy: Do not access, collect, or disclose another person's information without proper authorization or a valid legal basis.
  • Honesty: Do not falsify information, misrepresent digital work, or deceive others using technology.
  • Respect for Intellectual Property: Recognize the rights of creators and follow copyright and licensing requirements.
  • Responsibility: Consider the possible consequences of actions performed through computer systems.
  • Fairness: Avoid using technology to discriminate against or unfairly disadvantage others.
  • Respect: Communicate appropriately and avoid harassment, threats, and harmful online behavior.
  • Accountability: Accept responsibility for actions performed using digital accounts and systems.

Importance of Computer Ethics

  • Encourages responsible use of computers and the internet.
  • Protects the rights and privacy of other people.
  • Promotes honesty in academic and professional activities.
  • Discourages cyberbullying and digital misconduct.
  • Respects intellectual property and copyright.
  • Supports fairness and accountability in technology use.
  • Promotes trust in digital communication and information systems.

Ethical Issues in Computing

Ethical issues in computing are situations that involve decisions about the proper use of technology, information, software, and digital resources. These issues can affect individuals, organizations, and society.

Common Ethical Issues

  • Software Piracy: Copying, installing, distributing, or using software without the required permission or license.
  • Plagiarism: Presenting another person's words, ideas, code, or work as one's own without proper acknowledgment.
  • Unauthorized Access: Accessing another person's account, computer, network, or information without permission.
  • Cyberbullying: Using digital platforms to harass, threaten, embarrass, or intimidate another person.
  • Copyright Infringement: Using or distributing protected digital content without the required permission or legal basis.
  • Online Fraud: Using digital platforms to deceive people for money, information, or other benefits.
  • Misuse of Artificial Intelligence: Using AI tools to create deceptive content, impersonate others, violate privacy, or misrepresent work.
  • Digital Discrimination: Using technology in ways that unfairly disadvantage individuals or groups.
  • Software Manipulation: Intentionally changing software or digital records to deceive users or obtain unauthorized benefits.

Ethical Issues in Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence introduces additional ethical concerns because AI systems can collect and analyze large amounts of information and make predictions or recommendations that influence people's decisions.

  • Bias: AI systems may produce unfair results when their training data or design contains biases.
  • Privacy: AI applications may process personal information, photographs, recordings, or other sensitive data.
  • Transparency: Users may not understand how an AI system produces its results.
  • Accuracy: AI tools may generate incorrect, misleading, or fabricated information.
  • Accountability: Organizations must consider who is responsible when AI systems cause harm or produce unfair outcomes.
  • Intellectual Property: The use of copyrighted material in AI development and generated content can raise legal and ethical questions.

Responsible Use of Technology

Responsible technology use means using computers, mobile devices, applications, networks, and online services in a safe, respectful, legal, and ethical manner. Users should consider how their online actions may affect themselves and other people.

Rules for Responsible Computer Use

  • Respect other people's privacy and digital property.
  • Do not access files or accounts without permission.
  • Use properly licensed software and authorized resources.
  • Give credit to original authors and information sources.
  • Think carefully before posting or sharing information online.
  • Do not participate in cyberbullying or online harassment.
  • Verify information before sharing it with others.
  • Use AI tools honestly and follow academic or workplace rules.
  • Report suspicious activities and security problems.
  • Follow school, workplace, and applicable technology policies.

Digital Citizenship

Digital citizenship refers to the responsible and appropriate use of technology when participating in online communities. A good digital citizen understands online safety, respects other users, evaluates information carefully, and contributes positively to digital environments.

Examples of Ethical Computer Use

  • A student cites websites and books used in a research assignment instead of copying content without acknowledgment.
  • An employee does not share confidential company files with unauthorized people.
  • A programmer protects user information and avoids collecting unnecessary personal data.
  • A social media user asks permission before publishing another person's private photograph.
  • A computer user reports a discovered security vulnerability through an authorized reporting process.

Practical Applications

Cybersecurity, privacy, and computer ethics are applied in everyday activities involving computers, mobile devices, networks, and information systems. Their principles help individuals and organizations protect information and use technology responsibly.

In Education

  • Protecting student accounts and academic records.
  • Using school computers and networks responsibly.
  • Avoiding plagiarism in digital assignments.
  • Protecting online classroom and learning platform accounts.
  • Limiting access to student information to authorized staff.
  • Teaching students how to recognize phishing and online scams.

In Business

  • Securing customer and employee information.
  • Protecting financial and transaction records.
  • Controlling employee access to business systems.
  • Creating backup copies of important company data.
  • Monitoring systems for suspicious activities.
  • Establishing policies for responsible information handling.

In Healthcare

  • Protecting patient records and medical information.
  • Limiting access to authorized healthcare professionals.
  • Using secure systems for storing and transferring records.
  • Maintaining the availability of important healthcare systems.

In Government

  • Protecting citizens' personal information.
  • Securing government databases and online services.
  • Preventing unauthorized access to public records.
  • Maintaining the availability of essential digital services.

In Everyday Life

  • Using secure passwords for social media accounts.
  • Recognizing suspicious messages and online scams.
  • Protecting personal information when shopping online.
  • Reviewing privacy settings on digital platforms.
  • Respecting other people's privacy on social media.
  • Keeping personal devices and applications updated.

Example Scenario: Protecting a School Information System

A school maintains a database containing student profiles, grades, enrollment records, and contact information. To protect this database, the school implements user authentication, role-based access control, encrypted connections, regular backups, and security monitoring.

Students can view their own records, teachers can access the information required for their responsibilities, and authorized administrators can manage official academic records. These measures help maintain confidentiality, integrity, and availability while supporting responsible data processing.

Key Takeaways

  • Cybersecurity protects computers, networks, applications, and information from digital threats.
  • Information security follows the principles of Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.
  • Cybersecurity threats include hacking, malware, phishing, ransomware, and denial-of-service attacks.
  • Malware includes viruses, worms, Trojans, ransomware, spyware, and other malicious programs.
  • Authentication verifies identity, while authorization and access control determine permitted activities.
  • Encryption helps protect information from unauthorized reading.
  • Data privacy concerns the proper collection, processing, storage, use, and sharing of personal information.
  • The Data Privacy Act of 2012, or Republic Act No. 10173, protects personal information in the Philippines.
  • Computer ethics promotes honesty, respect, fairness, responsibility, and accountability.
  • Responsible technology use helps create a safer and more respectful digital environment.
  • Every computer user has a responsibility to protect information and respect the rights of others.
